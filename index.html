CPU ARCHITECTURE

Inside the machine

Everything you have built over the last five topics was a part. A gate. An adder. A flip-flop. A register. This is the assembly — the thing all those parts were parts of. And the honest surprise is that there is nothing new in here. A processor is the components you already understand, wired together and given a schedule. Press run and watch a program actually execute.

The orange block is real data moving on a real wire. Blue outline means a component is sending, amber means it is receiving, and the dashed purple lines are control signals coming from the control unit telling each part what to do.

Everything you built is in there

This is the part worth slowing down for. A processor is not a mysterious new object — it is six levels of the same thing, and you have personally built the bottom four. Click down through them.

A city, a street, a house, a brick

Look at a city from a plane and you see districts. Drop lower and you see streets. Lower again and it is individual houses, then rooms, then the bricks in a wall. Nobody thinks a city is made of something other than bricks — it is just bricks arranged at enormous scale. A CPU is the same. Zoom out and it is a chip that runs programs. Zoom all the way in and it is the XOR gate you flipped switches on in topic 2. Nothing was added along the way except arrangement.

If you take one thing from this topic, take this. There is no layer where the magic gets added. A processor running a video call is doing nothing that you cannot follow, all the way down to two transistors making a NOT gate. It is just very small, very fast, and there are a very great many of them. The cleverness is entirely in the arrangement, and the arrangement is what you have spent five topics learning.

The four parts of a CPU

Every processor ever made splits into the same four sections. Learn these four and the block diagram in any exam paper becomes readable.

A kitchen during service

The head chef reads the tickets and shouts who does what, but never cooks — that is the control unit. The line cooks actually chop and fry; that is the ALU. The counter space holding what is being worked on right now is the registers. And the runners carrying plates and ingredients between everybody are the buses. Take away any one of those four and service stops. Nobody in the kitchen is doing two jobs.

The mistake examiners look for. The control unit does not do calculations, and the ALU does not decide what to do next. If a question asks "what does the control unit do", the answer is decode instructions and generate control signals — never "performs arithmetic". Head chef does not cook.

The three buses

A bus is just a bundle of wires shared by several components. There are three of them and they do genuinely different jobs, which is why the exam keeps asking you to tell them apart.

Sending a parcel

Three separate things have to happen. You write the address on the box — that is the address bus, and it only ever goes one way, from the CPU outward. The box holds the contents — that is the data bus, and it goes both ways because you both send and receive. And somewhere there is an instruction saying whether this is a delivery or a collection — that is the control bus. Same parcel, three different kinds of information, and confusing them is how parcels end up in the wrong place.

Address bus width — try it

The address bus is why a 32-bit machine cannot use more than 4 GB of RAM, and it is a favourite exam calculation. Slide it and watch.

This is not a theoretical limit. Older 32-bit Windows machines genuinely could not use a fourth stick of RAM, no matter how much you spent. 2\u00b3\u00b2 is 4,294,967,296 \u2014 about 4 GB \u2014 and there simply were not enough wires to name an address beyond that. The whole industry moved to 64-bit addressing to escape it, which buys 16 exabytes and will last a while.

Open up the ALU

The arithmetic logic unit sounds like the clever bit. It is actually the most familiar bit — it is your adder from topic 3, a few logic gates, a multiplexer to choose between them, and some flip-flops to remember what happened.

A multi-tool, and the thing that picks the blade

A Swiss Army knife has a blade, a screwdriver, scissors and a bottle opener, all built in permanently. You do not add the scissors when you need them — they were always there. What you do is select one. An ALU is exactly that: the adder, the AND unit, the OR unit and the XOR unit are all wired up and all computing their answers simultaneously, every single cycle. A multiplexer then picks which answer is allowed out. Everything else is thrown away. It sounds wasteful until you realise selecting is instant and deciding first would be slow.

Flags (five flip-flops)
Flags are flip-flops, and that matters. Each flag is a one-bit register holding a fact about the last result: was it zero, was it negative, did it carry, did it overflow, was it even. They stay set until the next operation overwrites them \u2014 which is exactly the memory behaviour from topic 4. And they are how a program makes a decision: a conditional jump does nothing more than look at a flag and decide whether to overwrite the program counter.

Addressing modes

An instruction has to say where its data is. There are several ways to say that, and each one trades flexibility against speed. Same instruction, five different meanings.

Five ways to tell a friend where to meet you

"I'm right here" — no travelling at all, the answer is in the message. "Meet me at 12 Cayon Street" — a specific address, go straight there. "Ask my mother where I am" — you have to go one place to find out the real place. "Three doors down from where you are now" — worked out from your current position. "Flat 5, in the building you already know" — a base plus an offset. Every one of those gets your friend to you. They cost different amounts of walking.

Memory
How to tell immediate from direct in one second. Immediate means the number in the instruction is the data. Direct means the number in the instruction is an address where the data lives. LOAD #5 puts five in the accumulator; LOAD 5 goes to address five and brings back whatever is sitting there. The hash is doing a lot of work.

Cache, and why it works at all

Cache is a small pocket of very fast memory between the CPU and RAM. The interesting question is not what it is — it is why keeping a tiny copy helps so much.

The cookie jar on the counter

The cookies live in the pantry downstairs. Walking down takes a minute. So you bring a few up and keep them in a jar on the counter. Now here is the bit that makes it work: people do not eat one cookie. They eat one, then another, then another, from the same packet, within a few minutes. That habit is the whole reason the jar is worth having. Programs behave identically \u2014 they use the same variable repeatedly, and they read arrays in order. Cache is a bet on that habit, and it wins over ninety percent of the time.

Request some addresses and watch the hit rate. Try asking for the same few over and over, then try jumping about randomly.

Locality of reference \u2014 learn the phrase, it is the exam answer. Programs reuse the same data soon after using it (temporal locality) and use data stored nearby (spatial locality). Cache exploits both. Without those two habits, cache would be useless and every computer you own would be roughly ten times slower.
